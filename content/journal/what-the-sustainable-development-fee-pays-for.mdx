Every traveller to Bhutan pays a Sustainable Development Fee for each night they spend in the kingdom. It is the first thing people ask us about and often the thing they are least sure of. Is it a tax? A visa? A way of keeping people out?

It is none of those, exactly. It is the mechanism behind Bhutan's "high value, low volume" approach to tourism, a policy that dates back to the opening of the country in 1974. The idea was simple and, at the time, radical: rather than measure success by arrivals, measure it by what each visitor contributes and by what is left intact. Half a century later the dzong courtyards are quiet, the trails are empty, the forests are standing and the culture is alive. That is what the fee buys.

Here is where the money goes.

## Free healthcare

Bhutan's constitution guarantees free basic healthcare to every citizen, and the state delivers it: district hospitals, basic health units in remote valleys, and a traditional medicine system that sits alongside the modern one. Treatment that cannot be given in the country is paid for abroad. The SDF is one of the revenue streams that funds this, and in a country of fewer than 800,000 people with a small tax base it matters.

When you stay at a farmhouse in Bumthang, the family's grandmother was treated for free at the district hospital. That is not an abstraction.

## Free education

Schooling in Bhutan is free from primary level through to higher secondary, and the state funds university places for those who qualify. English is the medium of instruction, which is why your guide speaks it so well and why a village teenager in Trashigang can talk to you about the World Cup. Literacy has risen from a small minority to the large majority within two generations.

Tourism revenue, including the SDF, is explicitly earmarked by the government for health, education and infrastructure. The next generation of guides, physicians and foresters is in those classrooms.

## Conservation

Bhutan's constitution requires that at least 60 per cent of the country remain under forest cover in perpetuity. The actual figure is higher, around 70 per cent, and more than half the country is protected as national parks, sanctuaries and the biological corridors that join them. Tigers walk from the subtropical south to the alpine north along those corridors; snow leopards hunt above Laya; the black-necked cranes have a valley in Phobjikha where power lines were buried so the birds would not strike them.

Keeping all of that requires rangers, research, compensation for farmers whose crops are raided, and the political will not to log or dam what could be logged or dammed. The SDF funds part of it directly and makes the rest possible by giving the country an income that does not depend on extraction.

## Carbon negative

Bhutan is one of a handful of countries that absorbs more carbon than it emits. Its forests sequester several times the country's annual emissions, hydropower supplies almost all of its electricity, and the government has committed to remaining carbon neutral indefinitely. The forest-cover clause in the constitution is the reason that commitment is credible.

Your flight to Paro has a carbon cost, and we do not pretend otherwise. But the country you are flying into is a net sink, and the fee you pay to be there is helping to keep it one.

## What the fee is, in practice

The SDF is charged per person per night by the Royal Government of Bhutan, with a reduced rate for children aged six to eleven and an exemption for children under six. Indian nationals pay a separate rate in rupees. It is separate from the one-off visa fee. Every Dzong House journey price includes the SDF and the visa fee, so the number you see is the number you pay, and the current rates are set out on our FAQ page.

The fee has changed several times since 2022, when the kingdom reopened after the pandemic with a much higher rate and then adjusted it downward, and the government reviews it periodically. Verify against https://www.bhutan.travel before launch, and before you travel. We will always tell you the rate that applies to your dates.

## Why it matters to us

We named ourselves after the dzong, the fortress that guards a valley and holds its heart. The Sustainable Development Fee is Bhutan's dzong wall. It is what stands between the kingdom and the fate of every other beautiful place that opened its doors too wide.

When a traveller tells us the fee feels high, we ask them to walk through Punakha Dzong at four in the afternoon with nobody else in the courtyard, or to sit at Gangtey at dawn listening to the cranes. Then we ask what that is worth. Most of them already know.

Bhutan does not need more visitors. It needs the right ones, staying longer, going slower, and paying their way into a country that has decided what it wants to remain. We think that is the most honest deal in travel.
